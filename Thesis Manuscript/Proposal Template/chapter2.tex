\section{Review of Related Literature}

This chapter presents a comprehensive review of existing literature relevant to diabetes prediction using machine learning and explainable artificial intelligence, with particular emphasis on Philippine-specific contexts and datasets. The review is organized into six major themes: the burden of diabetes in the Philippines, available Philippine datasets for diabetes prediction, machine learning models for diabetes classification, explainable AI methods, calibrated explanations, and probability calibration techniques.

\subsection{Diabetes and Prediabetes as a Global and Philippine Health Concern}

Diabetes mellitus is a chronic metabolic disease characterized by elevated blood glucose levels resulting from inadequate insulin production, ineffective insulin utilization, or both. Persistent hyperglycemia can progressively damage the cardiovascular system, kidneys, eyes, and nerves, making diabetes an important contributor to morbidity and premature mortality worldwide \cite{who_diabetes_2026}. Type 2 diabetes, the most common form of the disease, is particularly significant from a prevention perspective because its onset can often be delayed or prevented through early identification of risk factors and appropriate lifestyle interventions \cite{who_diabetes_2026}.

Prediabetes represents an intermediate state in which blood glucose levels are higher than normal but have not yet reached the diagnostic threshold for diabetes. Individuals with prediabetes have an increased likelihood of progressing to type 2 diabetes, emphasizing the importance of identifying elevated risk before overt disease develops. Early detection and intervention are therefore central to diabetes prevention, particularly among individuals with modifiable risk factors related to diet, physical activity, body composition, and other metabolic characteristics \cite{who_diabetes_qa_2026}.

The burden of diabetes is also substantial in the Philippines. The International Diabetes Federation reported an adult diabetes prevalence of approximately 7.5\% in the Philippines for 2024 \cite{idf_philippines_2024}. National mortality statistics further demonstrate the seriousness of the disease. According to the Philippine Statistics Authority, diabetes mellitus accounted for 43,964 registered deaths in 2023 and remained among the country's leading causes of mortality \cite{psa_deaths_2023}. More recent provisional statistics for 2025 reported 41,656 deaths attributable to diabetes mellitus, ranking it fifth among the leading causes of death in the Philippines \cite{psa_deaths_2025}.

Philippine nutrition surveillance likewise indicates that abnormal blood glucose remains an important noncommunicable disease risk factor. The Department of Science and Technology--Food and Nutrition Research Institute incorporates diabetes and related metabolic indicators into the National Nutrition Survey and Expanded National Nutrition Survey, together with information on diet, anthropometry, physical activity, smoking, alcohol consumption, and other health-related characteristics \cite{fnri_enutrition}. These nationally collected variables provide an important basis for investigating diabetes risk within the specific demographic, dietary, and lifestyle context of the Filipino population.

Taken together, the continuing burden of diabetes and the possibility of preventing or delaying type 2 diabetes highlight the value of early risk identification. This motivates the development of data-driven prediction methods that can identify individuals who exhibit elevated diabetes risk before the onset of more severe disease. In the Philippine setting, the availability of ENNS data creates an opportunity to develop risk-prediction models based on locally representative health and nutrition information rather than relying exclusively on international diabetes datasets.

\subsection{Philippine Datasets for Diabetes Risk Prediction}

The availability and characteristics of Philippine datasets for diabetes risk prediction are crucial for developing locally relevant machine learning models. However, the literature reveals significant challenges and gaps in this domain.

The Department of Science and Technology-Food and Nutrition Research Institute (DOST-FNRI) conducts the Expanded National Nutrition Survey (ENNS), which provides comprehensive data on the eating habits, nutrition, and health status of Filipinos \cite{dostfnri_enutrition_puf}. The 2018–2020 Expanded National Nutrition Survey (ENNS) was implemented as a rolling survey across Philippine provinces and highly urbanized cities, covering 45,957 households in 2018 and 49,042 households in 2019 \cite{dostfnri_enns_2018_2019}. This dataset includes information on diabetes prevalence and related metabolic indicators \cite{dostfnri_enns_clinical}. According to the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) Diabetes Atlas, the age-adjusted comparative diabetes prevalence among Filipino adults aged 20--79 years was 7.1\% in 2021 \cite{idf_diabetes_atlas_2021}. While DOST-FNRI datasets are valuable for nutritional and epidemiological research, their Public Use Files (PUFs) are accessible through the eNutrition platform, which provides downloadable datasets and corresponding data dictionaries subject to data-use conditions \cite{dostfnri_enutrition_puf}.

Studies have utilized Philippine-specific datasets for diabetes prediction model development. One study optimized a bagged tree ensemble classifier to predict diabetes prevalence in women using a dataset of 1,888 participants. The model used six predictors: age, BMI, glucose level, insulin level, blood pressure, and pregnancy status \cite{candia_bagged_trees_2024}.

Recent research developed an automated machine learning (AutoML) model for referable diabetic retinopathy using 2,000 ultrawide field (UWF) retinal images from local Philippine datasets \cite{arcena_automl_2024}. The dataset was labeled according to the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) severity grading and split with 80\% for training, 10\% for validation, and 10\% for testing. The model achieved a sensitivity of 88\% (95\% CI: 0.80-0.94) and specificity of 83\% (95\% CI: 0.74-0.89) on external validation, approaching US FDA requirements for referable DR diagnosis.

A 2025 study employed a multi-method classification approach involving binomial logistic regression, K-means clustering, and decision tree analysis on 947 Filipino participants aged 24-79 years \cite{campugan_diabetes_2025}. The study identified BMI as the most significant predictor ($\chi^2$(1) = 104.44, p < .001), followed by HbA1c ($\chi^2$(1) = 51.80, p < .001), triglycerides ($\chi^2$(1) = 12.44, p < .001), and LDL ($\chi^2$(1) = 9.15, p = .002) \cite{campugan_diabetes_2025}. The model demonstrated excellent predictive performance with a McFadden $R^2$ of 0.80 and Nagelkerke $R^2$ of 0.85 \cite{campugan_diabetes_2025}. K-means clustering successfully categorized participants into two distinct groups based on biomarker profiles, differentiating those at higher and lower diabetes risk \cite{campugan_diabetes_2025}.

Despite these efforts, preprocessing considerations remain important when working with Philippine nutrition survey data. The ENNS is organized into multiple survey components with distinct variables and corresponding data dictionaries, requiring appropriate data preparation and variable selection before analysis \cite{dostfnri_enutrition_puf}.

\subsection{Machine Learning Models for Diabetes Prediction}

Machine learning approaches for diabetes prediction span a wide range of algorithms, from classical statistical methods to modern gradient boosting frameworks and deep learning architectures. Comparative research has demonstrated that model selection, hyperparameter optimization, and data preprocessing influence predictive performance on structured clinical datasets \cite{mao_diabetes_ml_2023, grinsztajn_tabular_2022}.

Gradient boosting frameworks have demonstrated competitive predictive performance across diabetes classification studies. Monalisha and Singh (2025) applied XGBoost with Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) and reported 96.4\% accuracy, 95\% precision, 96\% recall, an F1-score of 95.5\%, and an AUC of 0.98. The study identified HbA1c and blood glucose as important predictors \cite{monalisha_xgboost_2025}. Khurshid et al. (2025) further investigated Bayesian optimization for XGBoost, reporting 97.26\% accuracy and an F1-score of 95.72\%, compared with 97.24\% accuracy and the same F1-score using grid search. These findings demonstrated a modest improvement in accuracy through Bayesian optimization \cite{khurshid_xgboost_2025}. In a separate study involving 29,509 adults from the NHANES 2007--2018 dataset, Riveros Perez and Avella-Molano (2025) found that XGBoost achieved the highest AUC of 0.8168 among the evaluated algorithms, followed by Random Forest and Logistic Regression with AUC values of approximately 0.79 \cite{riveros_perez_diabetes_2025}. Random Forest also demonstrated competitive performance in diabetes prediction. Y\"uksel and G\"uzel (2025) reported an F1-score of 0.762 using all 21 features of the BRFSS dataset, while a reduced model using four features retained an F1-score of 0.751 \cite{yuksel_guzel_rf_2025}. Similarly, Alzboon et al. (2025) reported that Random Forest achieved 76.30\% accuracy, ranking second to a neural network model with 78.57\% accuracy on the Pima Indians Diabetes Dataset \cite{alzboon_diabetes_2025}.

K-Nearest Neighbors (kNN), while simpler in design, has demonstrated competitive results when combined with class balancing techniques. Ullah et al. (2022) applied the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique with Edited Nearest Neighbors (SMOTE-ENN) to the 2015 Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) dataset. Their kNN classifier achieved approximately 98.38\% accuracy, outperforming the other classifiers evaluated in the same experimental setting \cite{ullah_diabetes_2022}. Hameed and Joshi (2024) further investigated the influence of neighborhood size and distance measures on kNN performance using the Pima Indians Diabetes Dataset. The highest reported accuracy was 80.5\%, obtained with $k=35$ and Euclidean distance. Their findings emphasized the importance of selecting appropriate neighborhood sizes and distance metrics to improve classification performance \cite{hameed_joshi_knn_2024}.

Deep learning approaches have also been investigated for diabetes prediction. Bayesian-Optimized TabNet (BO-TabNet) combines TabNet's sequential attention mechanism, which enables instance-wise feature selection, with Bayesian hyperparameter optimization. Joseph et al. (2022) developed an explainable BO-TabNet architecture that achieved 92.2\% accuracy on the Pima Indians Diabetes Dataset and 99.4\% accuracy on the Early-Stage Diabetes Risk Prediction Dataset. The proposed model outperformed the benchmark classifiers evaluated in their experiments, while its attention mechanism and additional SHAP and LIME analyses supported model interpretation \cite{joseph_tabnet_diabetes_2022}. However, comparative research on tabular machine learning has shown that tree-based methods, including gradient boosting, remain competitive with or outperform deep learning architectures on many medium-sized structured datasets \cite{grinsztajn_tabular_2022}. These findings support the evaluation of both tree-based and neural-network-based approaches rather than assuming that one model family will consistently outperform the other.

Ensemble learning methods aim to improve predictive performance by combining the outputs of multiple base models. Soft voting classifiers aggregate predicted class probabilities, whereas stacking ensembles train a meta-learner using predictions generated by multiple base classifiers. Kibria et al. (2022) developed an explainable soft voting ensemble for diabetes classification using the Pima Indians Diabetes Dataset. The proposed weighted ensemble achieved 90\% balanced accuracy and an F1-score of 89\% under five-fold cross-validation \cite{kibria_soft_voting_2022}. In addition, Lu and Uddin (2022) investigated an explainable stacking-based model for predicting hospital readmission among patients with diabetes, illustrating the application of a meta-learning approach to a diabetes-related clinical prediction task \cite{lu_uddin_stacking_2022}. These findings demonstrate the potential of combining complementary learners while highlighting the need to evaluate ensemble performance within the specific dataset and prediction objective.

\subsection{Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) Methods}

Deploying machine learning models in clinical settings requires more than predictive accuracy. Clinicians need to understand the factors influencing model predictions, particularly when these predictions are intended to support clinical decision-making. Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) methods address this need by providing interpretations of model behavior at both global and individual levels. Recent diabetes prediction studies have applied post-hoc XAI techniques to improve the transparency of machine learning models and identify important factors contributing to their predictions \cite{kirbas_cifci_shap_2025, hasan_automl_xai_2025}.

SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) is a post-hoc explanation method grounded in cooperative game theory. SHAP values quantify the contribution of individual features to model predictions, supporting both local and global interpretation. K{\i}rba{\c{s}} and {\c{C}}ifci (2025) evaluated eight machine learning algorithms using the Pima Indians Diabetes Dataset and found that Random Forest achieved 82\% accuracy and an F1-score of 0.83 using a train-test split. Under 10-fold cross-validation, the model achieved 83\% accuracy and an F1-score of 0.84. SHAP analysis identified glucose, BMI, pregnancies, and insulin as influential predictors of diabetes classification \cite{kirbas_cifci_shap_2025}. These findings demonstrated how SHAP could identify important predictors across a dataset, while individual explanations could illustrate the direction and magnitude of feature contributions to specific predictions.

LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations) provides local explanations by approximating the behavior of a complex model around an individual observation using a simpler interpretable surrogate model. Hasan et al. (2025) integrated LIME and SHAP into an automated machine learning framework for diabetes prediction. Their LIME analysis identified BMI greater than 36.60 as contributing $+0.1622$ and diabetes pedigree function greater than 0.63 as contributing $+0.1490$ toward a particular prediction, while pregnancies of at most one contributed $-0.0673$ \cite{hasan_automl_xai_2025}. These findings illustrated LIME's ability to provide instance-specific explanations through feature contributions. However, LIME explanations depend on the locally generated samples and the surrogate model used for approximation. Hung and Lee (2024) examined the limitations of local linear approximations, particularly when explaining nonlinear decision boundaries, and developed BMB-LIME to improve local fidelity, stability, and uncertainty representation \cite{hung_lee_lime_2024}. These limitations highlighted the importance of assessing explanation reliability in healthcare applications.

Although SHAP and LIME improve the interpretability of machine learning predictions, their explanations do not inherently establish whether the underlying predicted probabilities correspond to observed outcome frequencies. This distinction is particularly important in clinical prediction, where explanation quality and probability reliability address different aspects of model evaluation. Riley et al. (2024) emphasized that clinical prediction models should be evaluated in terms of discrimination, calibration, and overall predictive performance \cite{riley_validation_2024}. Accordingly, the integration of explainability with probability calibration represents an important consideration for developing reliable diabetes-risk prediction systems. This provides the rationale for examining calibrated explanations, which incorporate uncertainty information alongside interpretations of individual predictions.

\subsection{Calibrated Explanations}

Calibrated Explanations (CE) is a framework developed by Lofstr\"om et al. \cite{lofstrom_ce_2024} that provides per-instance explanations with uncertainty bounds, using Venn-Abers calibration as its core probabilistic foundation. Unlike SHAP and LIME, which explain predictions after the fact without addressing whether those predictions are well-calibrated, CE integrates calibration directly into the explanation process so that each feature weight is accompanied by a confidence interval reflecting how stable that contribution is across the calibration data.

The original CE study evaluated the framework across 25 benchmark classification datasets and reported that CE produced more stable and reliable explanations than LIME and SHAP, with lower sensitivity to small input perturbations \cite{lofstrom_ce_2024}. The framework generates per-instance factual CE explanations, which show what features drove a given prediction and how certain those contributions are, and Calibrated Counterfactual Explanations (CCE), which identify the smallest feature changes that would shift a prediction across a defined risk threshold. This combination of factual and counterfactual outputs, together with uncertainty quantification, addresses practical gaps that SHAP and LIME leave open in clinical use cases.

A further refinement introduced in the same framework is Ensured CCE, which filters counterfactual alternatives to retain only those with sufficiently narrow uncertainty bounds \cite{lofstrom_ce_2024}. The distinction between standard CCE and Ensured CCE is meaningful in patient counseling, since only ensured alternatives can be confidently presented as actionable recommendations. A standard CCE might suggest that reducing waist circumference by 5 cm would lower a patient's predicted risk, but if the uncertainty interval around that suggestion is wide, the recommendation may not hold reliably. An Ensured CCE presents that suggestion only when the underlying prediction intervals confirm it with sufficient certainty.

Lofstr\"om et al. extended CE to regression tasks in a subsequent study \cite{lofstrom_regression_2025}, enabling uncertainty-bounded explanations when the target variable is a continuous biomarker rather than a binary class label. This extension is relevant for diabetes prediction when working with continuous measurements such as fasting blood glucose or HbA1c levels. A fast perturbation-based variant of CE was further developed to reduce computational overhead while preserving the uncertainty quantification property \cite{lofstrom_fast_ce_2026}, making real-time clinical deployment more feasible. These extensions reflect an active research trajectory around broadening the CE framework's applicability across different clinical prediction scenarios.

The CE framework also supports a Mondrian calibration variant that performs calibration within subgroups defined by a specific feature \cite{lofstrom_ce_2024}. Applied to diabetes risk prediction, this enables separate calibration quality evaluation for male and female patient groups, for example. Research applying conditional Mondrian calibration in medical settings has highlighted that subgroup-specific calibration can uncover disparities in model reliability, where one group may consistently receive wider prediction intervals, indicating lower certainty for that demographic. This type of subgroup analysis is an increasingly important component of fairness evaluation in clinical AI systems.
